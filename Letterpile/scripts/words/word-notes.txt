# Word page notes: word | part of speech | meaning | example sentence | note | status (draft or ok)

people | noun | Human beings in general, or the men, women and children of a particular group or place. It also works as a verb meaning to fill a place with inhabitants. | "Hundreds of people lined the street to watch the parade." | "People" is the usual plural of "person"; "persons" survives mainly in legal and formal writing. It comes from Latin "populus", the same root as "popular" and "population". In word games it uses two Ps and two Es, so it needs a generous rack. | ok
world | noun | The earth together with all its countries and people; also a particular area of life or activity, such as the world of sport. | "She saved for three years to travel around the world." | "World" goes back to Old English "weorold", roughly "the age of man". Its single vowel makes it a handy way to clear W, R, L and D in one play. Take away the R and you get "wold", an old word for open upland. | ok
life | noun | The state of being alive, or the period between birth and death. It can also mean the energy and liveliness a person or place has. | "Grandad lived his whole life within ten kilometres of the farm." | The plural is "lives", although "still lifes" is the accepted plural for paintings. Its letters rearrange to "file" and "lief", an old word meaning gladly. Change one letter and you get "wife", "lift" or "like", handy rungs in a word ladder. | ok
work | noun, verb | Effort done to achieve something, especially a job done for pay; as a verb, to do that effort, or for a machine or plan to function properly. | "The new heater finally works, so the office is warm again." | "Work" is one of the oldest words in English, with relatives across the Germanic languages. A huge family of compounds grows from it: homework, network, workshop, teamwork. W and K together make it a decent-scoring four-letter word. | ok
great | adjective | Very large in size, amount or importance; also, informally, very good. | "We had a great time at the beach, despite the wind." | "Great" once meant coarse or thick before it came to mean big, and "great-grandmother" keeps the sense of one step further. In Wordle-style games it tests G, R, E, A and T in one go. Don't confuse it with its anagram "grate", a frame for a fire or to shred food. | ok
home | noun | The place where someone lives, especially with family; also the place where something started or belongs. | "After a long week away, it was good to be home." | Compare "house", the building, with "home", the place you belong. "Home in on" (to close in on a target) is the standard phrase, while "hone in on" is a common slip. It has no anagrams, but it takes back hooks to make "homes", "homer" and "homey". | ok
house | noun, verb | A building where people live, usually a single household; as a verb, to provide space or shelter for someone or something. | "The museum houses one of the country's oldest waka." | Note the change in sound: the noun ends with an S sound, the verb with a Z sound. Three vowels (O, U, E) make "house" a reasonable Wordle-style opener. A house in Old English was "hus", which survives in "husband", the master of the house. | ok
place | noun, verb | A particular position, spot or area; as a verb, to put something in a certain position or to identify it. | "I know her face but I can't place where we met." | "Place" comes from Latin "platea", a broad street, which also gave us "plaza". In word games it's a good five-letter combination of P, L and C. Words like "replace" and "placement" grow straight from it. | ok
school | noun | A place where children are taught, or any institution for learning; also a large group of fish swimming together. | "A school of kahawai flashed past the boat." | The fish meaning comes from a different Dutch root than the classroom meaning, so the two are separate words that happen to look the same. The SCH spelling with a K sound trips up many learners. "Schools" and "schooled" are its everyday forms. | ok
family | noun | A group made up of parents and their children, or more widely, all the people related by blood or marriage; also a group of related things, such as languages. | "The whole family gathers at the bach every summer." | From Latin "familia", which originally meant the whole household, servants included. The Y ending counts as a vowel sound, so it's a six-letter word with only two true vowels. The plural is "families", changing Y to IES. | ok
money | noun | Coins and notes used to buy things, or wealth in general. | "They put a little money aside each week for the holiday." | The word comes from Juno Moneta, a Roman goddess whose temple housed the mint. Two plurals are accepted: "moneys" and "monies", the second mostly in legal or financial writing. In Wordle-style games, M, N and Y make it a tricky late guess. | ok
number | noun, verb | A quantity or figure such as 3 or 50, used for counting or measuring; as a verb, to give items numbers or to amount to a total. | "Please write your phone number on the form." | With a capital N, "Numbers" is a book of the Bible. "Numb-er" (more numb) is spelled the same, a neat trap for spelling quizzes. Its common abbreviation "No." comes from Latin "numero". | ok
city | noun | A large and important town, often with its own local government. | "Auckland is the country's largest city by population." | "City" shares a root with "citizen" and "civil", all from Latin "civis". In the UK, city status is granted rather than earned by size, which is why some small places are cities. Three letters plus Y make it a short way to play C. | ok
night | noun | The time of darkness between sunset and sunrise. | "The stars were bright on our first night at the campsite." | The silent GH is a leftover from Old English, which really did pronounce it. Its anagram "thing" makes the pair a favourite in letter puzzles. "Knight" is the same word plus a silent K, but the two aren't related in meaning. | ok
game | noun | An activity played for fun or competition, usually with rules; also wild animals hunted for food. As an adjective, it means willing to try something. | "Are you game for a swim before breakfast?" | The "willing" sense comes from the idea of a fighting cock that keeps going. Its letters rearrange to "mage". Daily puzzle games are a good way to practise spotting both. | ok
company | noun | A business that makes or sells things or services; also the fact of being with other people, or a group of performers. | "We enjoyed their company over a long dinner." | It literally means "people you share bread with", from Latin "com" (with) and "panis" (bread). The abbreviation "Co." is common in business names. With C, M, P and Y it scores well for a seven-letter word. | ok
business | noun | Buying and selling as a way of earning money, or a particular company; also a matter that concerns someone. | "Mind your own business." | It began as "busy-ness", the state of being busy, which explains the odd spelling with an I where you hear nothing. Its double S at the end catches out many spellers. It's eight letters long, the maximum length for Letterpile word pages. | ok
music | noun | Sounds arranged in a pleasing or expressive way, made with voices or instruments. | "She plays music in the car on the way to work." | From Greek "mousike", the art of the Muses. "Face the music" means to accept the consequences of something. Two vowels and the letters M, S and C make it a solid middle guess in a five-letter puzzle. | ok
power | noun, verb | The ability or strength to do something, control over others, or energy such as electricity; as a verb, to supply with energy. | "The power went out during the storm." | In maths, a power tells you how many times a number is multiplied by itself, so 2 to the power of 3 is 8. Its five letters include the useful W and P. It has no anagrams, but it builds into "powerful", "empower" and "powers". | ok
water | noun, verb | The clear liquid that falls as rain and fills rivers, lakes and seas; as a verb, to pour water on plants or for the eyes or mouth to produce liquid. | "Remember to water the tomatoes while we're away." | "Water" has barely changed in over a thousand years of English. "In hot water" means in trouble, and "water off a duck's back" means criticism that has no effect. In five-letter guessing games it tests W, T and R alongside two common vowels. | ok
friend | noun | A person you know well and like, who isn't family. | "My oldest friend still lives on the same street." | The spelling rule "I before E except after C" works here, and a handy memory aid is that you'll be friends to the END. It comes from an Old English word meaning "to love". Its anagrams include "finder" and "redfin". | ok
story | noun | An account of events, real or made up, told for entertainment or information; also a level of a building (often spelled "storey" in NZ and UK English). | "She read the children a story before bed." | NZ and UK English use "storey" for a floor of a building and "story" for a tale; American English uses "story" for both. The building sense may come from rows of painted windows that told stories. In word games its Y works as a vowel, and the plural changes Y to IES: "stories". | ok
health | noun | The condition of a person's body and mind, especially whether they are well or ill. | "Regular walks do wonders for your health." | It shares a root with "whole" and "heal"; good health once meant being whole. In word games, H appears twice and the vowel count is low, which can be useful for clearing consonants. "Healthy" and "healthier" are its common adjective forms. | ok
history | noun | The study of past events, or the whole series of events connected with a person, place or thing. | "The town's history goes back to the gold rush." | From Greek "historia", meaning inquiry or knowledge from asking around. "History" and "story" come from the same root. With only two vowels and a Y, it's an awkward seven-letter rack to build without the right letters. | ok
food | noun | Anything people or animals eat to stay alive and grow. | "There's plenty of food left over from the party." | The double O sounds different here from "flood" or "good", a classic spelling puzzle in English. "Food for thought" means something worth thinking about. Its back hook makes "foods", and "fool" is one letter away in a word ladder. | ok
body | noun | The physical structure of a person or animal; also the main part of something, or a group of people organised together. | "A regional body oversees water quality in the river." | "Body" can mean a corpse, a group of people (an organised body) or the main text of a document, so context matters. Its Y works as a vowel. In word ladders, "body" to "bony" is a one-letter change. | ok
office | noun | A room or building where people do administrative or professional work; also a position of authority or duty. | "She held the office of mayor for nine years." | From Latin "officium", meaning duty or service. Note the double F, a frequent misspelling. In word games, it's a six-letter way to use two Fs, which are worth four points each with standard tile values. | ok
problem | noun | A situation or matter that is difficult and needs to be dealt with or solved. | "If you have a problem with the booking, call the front desk." | From Greek "problema", something thrown forward, a task set in front of you. "No problem" has become a common way to say "you're welcome". With P, B and M, it's a strong-scoring seven-letter word that uses all its tiles. | ok
question | noun, verb | A sentence or phrase that asks for information; as a verb, to ask someone things, or to express doubt about something. | "I question whether the plan will work in winter." | It shares a root with "quest" and "query", from Latin "quaerere", to seek. It contains a Q followed by U, which makes the Q easy to play in word games. Its eight letters include four vowels, a good balance. | ok
morning | noun | The early part of the day, from sunrise to midday. | "The café opens at seven every morning." | Don't confuse it with "mourning", grieving for someone who has died; the sounds are identical in most accents. Its -ING ending is one of the most useful letter patterns in word games. "Mornings" is used as an adverb in phrases like "she works mornings". | ok
police | noun, verb | The organised force responsible for keeping public order and enforcing the law; as a verb, to keep order or enforce rules. | "Police closed the motorway after the crash." | It is usually treated as plural: "the police are investigating". It comes from Greek "polis", a city, the same root as "politics" and "metropolis". It has no anagrams, but "polices" and "policed" are valid verb forms. | ok
light | noun, adjective, verb | The natural energy that makes things visible, such as sunlight; as an adjective, not heavy or pale in colour; as a verb, to set something burning. | "Can you light the barbecue while I fetch the sausages?" | The silent GH turns up in a whole family of rhymes: fight, might, night, right, sight, tight. That family is a known trap in Wordle-style games, where -IGHT can leave you guessing the first letter for several turns. It has no anagrams in the list. | ok
reason | noun, verb | A cause or explanation for something; also the power of the mind to think and make judgements. As a verb, to think in a logical way. | "There's no reason to rush; the bus isn't due for twenty minutes." | It reached English through Old French "raison", from Latin "ratio". Its best-known anagram is "senora", the Spanish title for a married woman. With three vowels in six letters, it is an easy word to build from a balanced rack. | ok
future | noun, adjective | The time that is still to come; as an adjective, happening or existing later. | "In future, please lock the gate behind you." | NZ and UK English say "in future" (from now on) as well as "in the future" (at some later time). From Latin "futurus", about to be. In word games, its U appears twice, which can catch out solvers who avoid repeats. | ok
country | noun | A nation with its own government and land; also the countryside, away from towns. | "They moved from the city to the country to grow their own vegetables." | It comes from Latin "contra", meaning the land lying opposite or in front of you. Its OU spelling makes an "uh" sound, as in "cousin" and "double". The plural is "countries". | ok
child | noun | A young human below the age of puberty, or a son or daughter of any age. | "Even as a child, she loved building things." | The plural "children" is one of the few English plurals ending in -EN, along with "oxen" and "brethren". The letters C, H, L and D with one vowel make it a useful word for clearing a rack. "Childish" and "childlike" mean different things: the first is critical, the second warm. | ok
mind | noun, verb | The part of a person that thinks, feels and remembers; as a verb, to be bothered by something or to look after it. | "Would you mind the kids while I nip to the shops?" | "Mind the gap" and "never mind" keep the old sense of paying attention. "Minder" is a person who looks after someone. Word ladders run mind, mint, mine and kind, all one letter apart. | ok
idea | noun | A thought, plan or suggestion about what to do; also an understanding of something. | "I've no idea where I left my keys." | From Greek "idea", meaning form or pattern. Three vowels in four letters make it very handy for clearing vowels in word games. Its anagram "aide" is a person who helps. | ok
change | noun, verb | To make or become different; as a noun, the act of becoming different, or coins and the money returned when you pay too much. | "Traffic is heavy, so we might change our route home." | From Latin "cambiare", to exchange. In word games, the CH and G make it a good-scoring six-letter word. "Changeable" keeps the E before -ABLE to keep the G soft. | ok
point | noun, verb | The sharp end of something, a particular place or moment, or the main idea in what someone says; as a verb, to direct a finger or object towards something. | "She made a good point about the cost." | In sport and games, points are units of score, and "point" also names the full stop in numbers, as in 2.5. "Point out" means draw attention to. Its letters rearrange to "pinto" and "piton", and the OI vowel pair is useful in five-letter puzzles. | ok
order | noun, verb | The way things are arranged, a command, or a request for goods; as a verb, to tell someone to do something or to ask for something to be supplied. | "We ordered fish and chips for the whole table." | "In order to" can usually be shortened to "to". The word comes from Latin "ordo", a row or rank. Two Rs and one O make it a slightly awkward five-letter word in puzzles. | ok
party | noun | A social gathering for fun, a political group, or one person or side in an agreement or dispute. | "Both parties signed the contract." | All senses come from "part": a group of people is part of a larger whole. In legal writing, "party" means a person or side in an agreement, as in "third party insurance". In word games the Y acts as a vowel; the plural is "parties". | ok
black | adjective, noun | The darkest colour, like the night sky with no light; as a noun, the colour itself. | "The All Blacks wear black from head to toe." | "In the black" means having money, while "in the red" means owing money. Its five letters use only one vowel, A. In Wordle-style games, the CK ending is worth testing. | ok
white | adjective, noun | The colour of fresh snow or milk; as a noun, the colour itself, or the clear part of an egg around the yolk. | "Separate the whites from the yolks." | "White lie" means a harmless untruth, and a "white elephant" is a costly, useless possession. In most NZ accents the H in WH is silent, so "white" and "wight" sound alike. Its anagram "withe" is a flexible twig used for tying. | ok
heart | noun | The organ that pumps blood around the body; also the centre of something, or feelings and courage. | "The café is in the heart of town." | "By heart" means learned so well you can say it without looking, and "heart of gold" means very kind. Its anagrams "earth" and "hater" make it a word-game classic. It is also a good Wordle-style opener, testing H, E, A, R and T. | ok
earth | noun | The planet we live on, or the soil in which plants grow; in electrics, a wire that connects equipment safely to the ground. | "Make sure the appliance is properly earthed." | NZ and UK English say "earth" for the safety wire where American English says "ground". Its anagram "heart" is a puzzle favourite. Capitalise "Earth" when it means the planet. | ok
plant | noun, verb | A living thing that grows in soil and makes its own food from sunlight, such as a tree or flower; also a factory. As a verb, to put seeds or plants in the ground. | "We planted pōhutukawa along the fence line." | The factory sense, as in a power plant, comes from the idea of something fixed in place. In five-letter puzzles, it tests P, L, N and T with a single A. Its letters don't make another word. | ok
sound | noun, verb, adjective | Something you can hear; as a verb, to make a noise or seem a certain way; as an adjective, healthy, solid or sensible. | "That sounds like a sound plan." | A sound is also a narrow stretch of sea, as in Milford Sound, a separate word from Old Norse. The OU vowel pair is useful in five-letter guessing games. "Sound asleep" means deeply asleep. | ok
smile | noun, verb | To turn the corners of your mouth up to show happiness or amusement; as a noun, that expression. | "She smiled when she saw the surprise." | Its anagrams make it a puzzle favourite: "limes", "miles", "slime" and "smile" all use the same letters. Fun fact: "miles" is sometimes jokingly called the longest word, with a mile between the first and last letters. | ok
dream | noun, verb | A series of images and feelings that happen in the mind during sleep; also a hope or ambition. As a verb, to have a dream. | "She dreams of sailing around the world." | Both "dreamed" and "dreamt" are correct past forms; "dreamt" is common in NZ and UK English and is one of the few English words ending in -MT. "Dream on" is a casual way of saying something won't happen. Its anagram "armed" makes it a tidy unscramble. | ok
peace | noun | Freedom from war or violence, or a calm, quiet state of mind. | "We finally have some peace and quiet." | Don't confuse it with "piece", a part of something; remember "a piece of pie". "Peace" uses three vowels in five letters, a useful pattern for word games. | ok
trust | noun, verb | A firm belief that someone or something is reliable, honest or able; as a verb, to have that belief. | "I trust you to lock up when you leave." | A trust is also a legal arrangement where property is held for someone else, as in a family trust. With two Ts and one vowel, it's a five-letter guess that rules out letters quickly. | ok
chair | noun, verb | A seat for one person with a back and usually four legs; also the person who runs a meeting. As a verb, to lead a meeting. | "She'll chair the meeting on Tuesday." | "Chair" in the meeting sense is a neutral alternative to "chairman" or "chairwoman". Its CH opening and the vowel pair before the R make it a good five-letter test word. | ok
table | noun, verb | A piece of furniture with a flat top and legs; also a set of facts or figures arranged in rows and columns. As a verb, to present something for discussion. | "The proposal was tabled at the meeting." | Note the split: in NZ and UK English, to "table" a motion is to put it forward for discussion, while in American English it means to put it aside. Its anagram is "bleat". | ok
happy | adjective | Feeling or showing pleasure or contentment. | "We're happy to help with the move." | "Happy" comes from "hap", an old word for luck, which also gives us "happen" and "mishap". The double P and the Y make it an awkward five-letter guess. It has no anagrams. | ok
raise | verb, noun | To lift something up, to increase an amount, or to bring up children; as a noun, an increase in pay (often called a "rise" in NZ and UK English). | "She raised her hand to ask a question." | "Raise" takes an object (you raise something), while "rise" doesn't (the sun rises). Its anagram "arise" is equally useful. It's widely considered one of the best opening words for Wordle-style games. | ok
arise | verb | To happen or start to exist; also, in older use, to get up. | "Problems can arise if you skip the instructions." | Its past forms are "arose" and "arisen". Its anagram "raise" makes the pair a good teaching example of letter order. With three vowels, "arise" is a popular first guess in five-letter puzzles. | ok
learn | verb | To gain knowledge or skill by studying, practising or being taught. | "He learned to swim at the local pools." | Both "learned" and "learnt" are correct; "learnt" is common in NZ and UK English. Its anagram "renal" relates to the kidneys. In five-letter games, it tests L, E, A, R and N, five of the most common letters. | ok
teach | verb | To show or explain to someone how to do something, or to give lessons in a subject. | "She teaches maths at the local intermediate." | The past tense is "taught", one of the irregular forms that trip up learners. Its letters also spell "tache", a casual word for a moustache, and "theca", a casing in biology. In five-letter games it tests the EA and CH pairs together. | ok
write | verb | To form letters or words on paper or a screen, or to create a book, song or other text. | "Write your name on the top of each page." | The silent W is a leftover from Old English, which pronounced it. The past forms "wrote" and "written" are irregular. In word ladders, "write" is one letter from "white". | ok
speak | verb | To say words aloud, or to have the ability to use a language. | "Do you speak te reo Māori?" | The past forms are "spoke" and "spoken", and "spake" is an old form that still appears in word lists. "Speak up" can mean speak louder or say what you think. Its anagram "peaks" makes the pair a quick unscramble. | ok
think | verb | To use your mind to consider something, or to have a certain belief or opinion. | "I think it might rain later." | The past form "thought" is irregular. A "think tank" is a group of experts who research ideas and policy. It has no anagrams, but "thank", "thick" and "thing" are one letter away. | ok
believe | verb | To accept that something is true, or to have faith in someone or something. | "I believe she's right." | The "I before E except after C" rule works here. "Believe" holds three Es, a challenge in word games that limit letter counts. "Make-believe" means pretending. | ok
remember | verb | To bring something back into your mind, or to keep it there and not forget it. | "Remember to bring a jacket." | From Latin "rememorari", to call to mind again. "Remember to do" means not forgetting a future task, while "remember doing" means recalling something past. With three Es and two Ms, it is a good test of letter counts in word puzzles. | ok
enough | determiner, adverb | As much or as many as needed; to the required degree. | "There's enough food for everyone." | The GH sounds like F here, one of English's best-known spelling quirks, as in "tough" and "rough". "Enough is enough" means you have reached your limit. With only two vowels in six letters, it is useful for clearing awkward consonants. | ok
little | adjective | Small in size or amount, short in time or distance, or young. | "A little help goes a long way." | "A little" (some) and "little" (not much) mean quite different things. The double T and LE ending make it a useful word for practising spelling. | ok
small | adjective | Not large in size, amount or importance. | "Could I get a small flat white, please?" | "Small talk" means light, polite conversation, and "the small hours" are just after midnight. "Small" and "little" overlap, but "little" often carries feeling ("a little house") while "small" is neutral. Its double L and single vowel make it a sharp five-letter test word. | ok
large | adjective | Big in size, amount or extent, or more than average for its kind. | "A large crowd turned up for the opening." | "At large" means free or not yet caught, and "by and large" means generally. In NZ and UK shops, "large" is a size, but "big" sounds more natural in everyday talk. Its anagrams include "lager", "glare" and "regal", a rich set for unscramble practice. | ok
young | adjective, noun | Having lived for only a short time; as a noun, the offspring of an animal. | "Young people often lead the way with new technology." | The OU here sounds like "uh", as in "touch" and "double". "Youngster" and "youth" come from the same root, but "youth" can mean a single young person or young people in general. In Wordle-style games, Y, U and G together make it a hard word to land without a hint. | ok
early | adjective, adverb | Happening near the start of a period of time, or before the expected time. | "The early bird catches the worm." | "Early bird" means someone who starts before others, and an "early night" means going to bed sooner than usual. As an adverb it keeps the same form: you arrive early, not "earlily". Its anagrams "layer" and "relay" make it a neat unscramble. | ok
simple | adjective | Easy to understand or do, or plain and without decoration. | "The recipe is simple: flour, water and salt." | From Latin "simplex", meaning single or one-fold. Its anagram "impels" means drives forward. "Keep it simple" is good advice for a first guess, too: test common letters. | ok
strong | adjective | Having great physical power, or hard to break, change or resist. | "A strong southerly blew all night." | "Strength" is its noun form and "strengthen" its verb. "Going strong" means still doing well, and a "strong point" is something you are good at. Its six letters hold only one vowel, so it is useful for clearing consonants in word games. | ok
coffee | noun | A hot drink made from roasted, ground coffee beans, or the beans themselves. | "Let's grab a coffee before the meeting." | NZ and Australia both claim the flat white, now served worldwide. The double F and double E make it an unusual six-letter word. It comes, via Turkish and Italian, from Arabic "qahwa". | ok
garden | noun, verb | A piece of ground where flowers, fruit or vegetables are grown; as a verb, to look after one. | "Every spring they plant tomatoes along the back of the garden." | "Lead someone up the garden path" means to mislead them, and a "garden variety" thing is ordinary. NZ and UK English say "garden" where Americans often say "yard". Its anagrams "danger" and "gander" make it a favourite in unscramble puzzles. | ok
weather | noun, verb | The state of the air outside, such as sun, rain, wind or temperature; as a verb, to come safely through a difficult time. | "We weathered the storm and kept going." | Don't confuse it with "whether", meaning if, or "wether", a castrated ram, familiar on NZ farms. "Under the weather" means feeling unwell. Its anagram "wreathe" means to surround or encircle. | ok
winter | noun | The coldest season of the year, which in NZ runs from June to August. | "Winter brings snow to the Southern Alps." | In NZ, winter runs from June to August, so the ski season in Queenstown and on Ruapehu falls mid-year. "Winter" can also be a verb: birds and stock "winter" somewhere. In word games, its common letters make it an easy six-letter play. | ok
summer | noun | The warmest season of the year, which in NZ runs from December to February. | "Summer holidays mean long days at the beach." | In NZ, summer covers Christmas and the long school holidays, so it is the season of baches and barbecues. The double M is a frequent spelling slip ("sumer"). "Summery" with a Y means summer-like, while "summary" with an A is a short account. | ok
spring | noun, verb | The season after winter, a coiled piece of metal that returns to shape, or a place where water flows from the ground; as a verb, to jump suddenly. | "In spring, the lambs arrive on the farm." | In NZ, spring runs from September to November, the opposite of the Northern Hemisphere. The verb has irregular past forms: "sprang" (it sprang open) and "sprung" (it has sprung a leak). In five-letter-plus puzzles, SPR is a common opening cluster worth testing. | ok
autumn | noun | The season between summer and winter, when leaves fall; in NZ it runs from March to May. | "The vineyards turn gold in autumn." | American English mostly says "fall"; "autumn" is the everyday word in NZ and the UK.
