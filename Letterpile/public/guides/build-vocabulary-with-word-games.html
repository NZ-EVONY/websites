Building Your Vocabulary with Word Games

Last updated: October 29, 2026

Most people learn new words the same way they learned their first ones: by meeting them again and again in a setting that gives them meaning. Word games are good at this, because they make you notice words, pull them out of memory and use them under a little pressure. But playing alone is not enough; a word you meet once in a puzzle is usually gone by the next day. This guide explains what actually makes new words stick, and sets out a short daily routine built around free puzzles.

What makes a new word stick

Research on learning points to a few ideas that matter far more than the number of words you see:

  • Retrieval beats rereading. Trying to recall a word, and sometimes failing, fixes it in memory more firmly than looking at it again. Puzzles are retrieval practice in disguise: a crossword clue asks you to pull the word out yourself.
  • Spacing beats cramming. Seeing a word on several separate days works better than seeing it many times in one sitting. A little every day suits this perfectly.
  • Context beats lists. A word learned with an example sentence and a reason to care about it lasts longer than one learned from a bare definition.
  • Use beats recognition. Recognising a word when you read it is the first step. Being able to use it is the real goal, and that needs you to say or write it a few times.

A ten-minute daily routine

You do not need hours. This routine takes about ten minutes, and each step uses one of the ideas above.

  1. Read the word of the day (1 minute). Letterpile's Word of the Day gives one word with a meaning, an example sentence and a short note on where it comes from or how it is used. Read the example out loud.
  2. Play the daily crossword (5 minutes). The Daily Crossword is retrieval practice: every clue asks you to recall a word from a definition. When you finish, or get stuck, look closely at any answer you did not know.
  3. Play a letter game (2 minutes). The five-letter word game or the Letterpile pile game make you search your memory for words that fit a pattern, which builds the links between spelling and meaning.
  4. Write down one new word (1 minute). Pick one word you met today that you would like to own. Write it in a notebook or on your phone with a sentence of your own that uses it.
  5. Review yesterday's word (1 minute). Cover the meaning and see if you can recall it. If you can, move it to a weekly review list; if you cannot, keep it on the daily list one more day.

That last step is a simple form of spaced repetition. Words you know move to longer gaps; words you keep forgetting come back sooner.

Getting more out of each puzzle

  • Look up the words you guessed. Answers you got only from crossing letters are the most useful ones to study, because you were close to knowing them. Letterpile's word pages, such as choice or heart, give a meaning, an example and related anagrams on one page.
  • Notice word families. When you learn a word, look at its relatives. Knowing that "-ology" means the study of something, or that "pre-" means before, unlocks dozens of words at once. The guide to prefixes and suffixes covers the common ones.
  • Play with anagrams. Rearranging the letters of a word you have just learned is a quick way to look at it closely. Try it in the anagram solver, or read anagram basics for the tricks.
  • Use rhyme for memory. A word that rhymes with something you know is easier to recall. The rhyming words tool helps find a hook.

Choosing which words to learn

Not every new word is worth the effort. A rare word that only appears in puzzle grids is fun to know but will not change how you read or write. A good rule is to favour words that you have now seen twice, or that fill a gap, meaning you have wanted to say something and lacked the word. Words with several meanings are also good value: learning that "fair" can mean just, pale, a festival or reasonably good gives you four uses for one entry.

For people learning English as an additional language, the most useful words early on are the common ones with tricky meanings, such as phrasal verbs (put up with, take off, turn down) and words that look alike but differ, such as quite and quiet, or affect and effect. Short daily puzzles are a low-stress way to meet these in context.

Word games for children

The same ideas work for children, with a lighter touch. Shared puzzles are best: solving a crossword clue together, or racing to find words in a word search, turns vocabulary into a game rather than a test. Ask what a word might mean before looking it up, and praise good guesses even when they are wrong; a sensible guess shows they are using the clue and the context, which is exactly the skill that builds reading. Letterpile's printable puzzles are handy for car trips and rainy afternoons away from screens.

Tracking your progress

Vocabulary grows slowly, so it is easy to feel you are not improving. A few signs that the routine is working:

  • You finish the daily crossword with fewer looked-up answers than a month ago.
  • Words from your notebook start turning up in things you read, and you recognise them straight away.
  • You use a new word in conversation or writing without stopping to think about it.

Keep the notebook for a couple of months and count the words you now use comfortably. Even one new word a day adds up to several hundred a year, and that is a noticeable difference in how precisely you can read, write and talk.
