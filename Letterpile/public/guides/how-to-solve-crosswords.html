How to Solve Everyday Crosswords

Last updated: October 29, 2026

An everyday crossword, sometimes called a quick or straight crossword, gives a plain definition for each answer. There is no hidden wordplay, but there is still a craft to it: clue wording carries more information than it first appears, crossing letters do much of the work, and a few habits make the difference between a half-empty grid and a finished one. This guide covers where to start, how to read clues closely, how to use the grid, and how to get better week by week.

Start with the easy wins

Nobody solves a crossword from 1 Across in order. Read through all the clues quickly and fill in anything you are sure of. Good first targets are:

  • Fill-in-the-blank clues, such as "Once in a blue ___ (4)". They usually have one obvious answer: MOON.
  • Short answers. Three- and four-letter answers have fewer possibilities, and each one gives you letters in two or more longer answers.
  • Clues you know cold, such as a capital city, a common abbreviation or a word you use every day.
  • Plurals and verb endings. If a clue is plural, the answer often ends in S; if it is in the past tense, the answer often ends in ED. Pencil the ending in even before you know the word.

Once a few answers are in, work outwards from them. A corner with several letters already placed is far easier than an empty one.

Read the clue the way the setter wrote it

Crossword clues follow a strict grammar rule: the clue and the answer must be interchangeable in a sentence. That gives you free information.

  • Part of speech. "Ran quickly (6)" wants a past-tense verb such as DASHED or RACED, not a noun.
  • Number. "Small rivers (6)" is plural: CREEKS, or perhaps BROOKS.
  • Tense and form. "Swimming" in a clue may want an -ING word; "swimmer" wants a person.
  • Abbreviations. If the clue contains an abbreviation or the word "for short", the answer is usually shortened too: "Doctor, for short (2)" is DR.
  • Question marks. A question mark at the end warns you that the clue is a pun or is not meant literally. "Bank job?" might be TELLER rather than ROBBERY.
  • Other languages. A clue that names a language or uses a foreign word, such as "Friend, in Paris (3)", wants an answer in that language: AMI.

Let the grid do the work

Every square in a crossword grid belongs to an across answer, a down answer or both. Each answer you place is a clue to its neighbours, so use them:

  1. Write down the pattern. If a six-letter answer reads ?R??G?, say it to yourself with the gaps. Patterns jog the memory better than the clue alone.
  2. Think about likely letter combinations. English words follow habits. A Q is almost always followed by U. An answer ending in ??ING or ??TION is common. If a down answer gives you a J or a Z, it narrows the across answer sharply.
  3. Check against the crossers. If an answer fits the clue but clashes with a crossing letter, one of the two is wrong. Re-read both clues before changing anything.
  4. Use a solver for a stuck pattern. When you have most of the letters and still cannot see it, the crossword solver lists every word that fits the known letters. Use it on one answer at a time; the crossing letters it unlocks will often let you finish the rest yourself.

Short answers that come up again and again

Every grid needs short words to tie the long ones together, and some short words appear far more often than in everyday English because their letters are so convenient. Regular solvers get to know them. Examples include ERA (age or period), ORE (rock with metal in it), ALOE (soothing plant), ODE (poem), EKE (stretch out, as in eke out), OBOE (woodwind instrument), EMU (large flightless bird) and AREA (region). If a three- or four-letter answer is full of vowels and common consonants, one of these is a strong first guess.

You can look any of these up on Letterpile's word pages, for example area, which give a plain meaning, an example and the word's anagrams.

When you are stuck

  • Leave it and come back. The answer often appears once you have looked away. Solve elsewhere in the grid and return with more letters.
  • Try a different meaning. Many clue words have more than one meaning. "Bark" might be a dog's sound or a tree's covering; "Lead" might be a metal or a dog's leash. Setters choose the less obvious one on purpose.
  • Say the clue out loud. Hearing a phrase can bring the answer to mind, especially for idioms and fill-in-the-blank clues.
  • Check your spelling. A wrong letter in one answer blocks every answer it crosses. If a corner will not come together, the fault is often a spelling slip you have already written in.
  • Reveal one letter, not the whole answer. If the puzzle allows it, revealing a single square teaches you more than revealing a word, and keeps the rest of the grid yours to solve.

How to get faster

Speed comes almost entirely from familiarity: knowing the common short answers, recognising the setter's habits and having a broad vocabulary. A few minutes every day builds this much faster than a long session once a week. Some practical habits:

  • Play the same daily puzzle regularly, so the style becomes familiar. Letterpile's Daily Crossword is a new 15×15 grid every day, with hand-written clues.
  • When you finish, look again at any answer you got only from crossing letters. Learning why the clue fitted is how you get it straight away next time.
  • Keep a short note of new words. The Word of the Day adds one a day with a meaning and an example.
  • Mix in other word puzzles. A word search trains you to spot letter patterns, and the daily five-letter word game trains you to reason from partial letters, which is exactly what crossing letters give you.

Moving on to cryptic crosswords

Once everyday crosswords feel comfortable, cryptic crosswords are the natural next step. Their clues hide a definition inside a piece of wordplay, which sounds daunting but follows a small number of rules. Our beginner's guide to cryptic crosswords explains them with worked examples.
